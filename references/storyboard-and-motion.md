# Storyboard, concrete imagery, and motion

## Shot design

For each lyric unit, define:

- what changes from the previous shot;
- the concrete subject and action;
- the emotional purpose;
- the dominant depth or camera scale;
- how the lyric enters and exits;
- the exact transition cue.

Good shot descriptions use verbs: reaches, hides, opens, splits, misses, turns away, crosses, grows, erases, reveals. “Character + background + particles” is an asset list, not a shot.

## Balance concrete and abstract material

Use abstract material for compression, punctuation, memory, distortion, or conceptual lines. Pair it with concrete anchors:

- a specific place;
- an identifiable prop;
- a character decision or reaction;
- a visible cause and consequence;
- a spatial journey.

During review, flag any sequence where multiple consecutive shots could be replaced by the same generic text card without changing the meaning. That is usually abstract filler.

## Avoid slideshow grammar

A still can feel cinematic when at least one relationship changes: foreground occludes the subject, the camera reveals information, a prop completes an action, time changes, depth separates, or typography interacts with the scene.

Repeated slow zoom, random crop, shake, flash, and chromatic aberration are effects, not progression. Use them only when motivated by sound or meaning.

## Instrumental sections

Give each instrumental a miniature arc:

1. establish or recall a concrete motif;
2. transform its scale, place, ownership, or condition;
3. resolve into the next vocal entry.

Examples include empty location to occupied location, intact prop to broken prop, childhood object to adult counterpart, observation screen to real landscape, or a repeated corridor whose exit finally appears.

## Complex actions

Before animating a walk, crawl, dance, turn, embrace, or object interaction, check whether the source art contains enough poses and separable layers.

If not, choose one of these:

- pose-to-pose match cuts;
- a sequence of detail inserts;
- anticipation and aftermath with the action omitted;
- silhouette or shadow animation;
- new key poses;
- environmental motion that carries the emotional action;
- a symbolic spatial transition such as ages aligned on successive steps.

Do not describe these substitutes as full character animation.

## Motion variety map

Track the dominant motion of each shot: static hold, pan, push, pull, orbit, lateral follow, parallax, wipe, match cut, object animation, typography-led motion, or full character action. Avoid repeating the same dominant move in adjacent shots unless repetition is the point.

Use stillness intentionally before high-energy sections. Constant motion flattens contrast and creates fatigue.
